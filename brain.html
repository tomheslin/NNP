Vital Signs

Normal Vital Signs

Temperature (axillary)

* 36.5-37.2 C axillary

* Rectal temps do not reflect core body temps and can be dangerous in the newborn.

Heart rate (Apical)

* 120-160 beats/minute

- Bradycardia * < 80 beats/minute

- Tachycardia * > 180 beats/minute

Respiratory rate

* 40-60 breaths/minute

  • Tachypnea

    • > 60 breaths/minute

  • Apnea

    • Cessation of respiration > than 20 seconds, which may occur with color change and or bradycardia.

  • Periodic Breathing

    • Cessation of respirations for up to 15 seconds followed by bursts of fast irregular respirations.

Blood Pressure Normals:

- Premature * > 35 mm Hg Systolic

- Term Infants * > 40 mm Hg Systolic

- Hypertension * >90 torr in Term Infants

*> 85 torr in Preterm Infants

Systolic high if > 15 + Gestational Age

Blood Pressure Mean: Should Approximate Gestational age + 7

Pulse pressure is wide if Mean B/P is > 25 mm Hg or (if > 1/2 Systolic).

Central Venous Pressure

  • Maintaining CVP at 5 to 8 mmHg with volume infusions is associated with improvement in cardiac output.

  • If CVP > than 5 to 8 mmHg, additional volume usually isn’t helpful.

  • CVP norm = 3-7 Torr

  • CVP is influenced by non-cardiac factors, such as ventilator pressures, and by cardiac factors, such as tricuspid valve function.

Fat Guidelines

* Calories derived from fat shouldn’t exceed more than 40% of total calorie intake. (parenteral)

* Interlipids can interfere with platelet production, note if platelets < 60,000.

* Infants who are < 1500g, pre-term, or SGA requires 2.5-3.0 gm/kg/day.

Fluid Advancement in Term Infants

  • Start with 60-80 cc/kg/day

  • Usually increasing by 20-30cc/kg/day

  • Increase to 150 cc/kg/day

Fluid Advancement in Preterm Infants

  • Infants < 700 grams usually require fluids in excess of 120-140 mL/kg/d

  • Infants between 800-1000 g, begin fluids at a rate of 80-100 mL/kg

  • Usually increasing by 10-20cc/kg day

  • Increase to 100-150cc/kg/day

  • Provide IV fluid replacement to maintain serum Na between 130-150cc/kg/day

  • Parental requirement 20% less than enteral

  • Adequate weight gain12gm/kg

Hydration

  • Fluids liberalized after the first few days to maintain UOP > 0.5 cc/kg/hr and urine specific gravity 1.012-1.016.

  • UOP & specific gravity are not adequate indicators of hydration during the first week.

  • First few days of life, electrolytes should be measured Q 6-8 hr.

  • Daily Weights, strict intake and output, urine specific gravity, and urine dipstick should be obtained q shift.

  • Serum glucose test strip as determined by NNP/ Physician

  • Hyperammonemia

  • Metabolic acidosis, (ph < 7.3, base excess, -6)

  • Azotemia

  • Hyperaminoacideemia

Calculations of Input and Output

*Input:

a. Number of ccs infused/24 hrs divided by wt (kg) = cc/kg/d input

*Output

b. Ccs of urine divided wt (kg) divided by number of hrs. = cc/kg/hr

Normal Urine Output (VLBW)

  • 1 mL/kg/hr on the first day of life, increasing to 5 to 7mL/kg/hr

  • Day of life 3 normal urine output is 2 to 4 mL/kg/hr

  • Urine specific gravity 1.003-1.015

  • Urine pH: 4.5-5.2 (normal renal function)

  • Suspect renal pathology if no voiding by 48hr: If by 72hr, little doubt of renal pathology.

Normal Stool Output

Extremely premature may have little or no stool output the first week of life.

  • Healthy full-term, passage of stool occurs within 24 hours of birth (In 95%)

  • Meconium is passed for the first 2 or 3 days, followed by transition and then fecal stools by 7th day.

  • Preterm infants may have delay in passage of stool

  • Delay shown to be inversely related to gestation age

Fluid/Lyte Regime for BPD

  • Adequate nutrition within restricted fluid vol.

  • 130-150 kcal/kg/day

  • Serum Na >125 mEq/L

  • Check electrolytes once/twice weekly

  • Check bimonthly X-rays for bone mineralization along with alk. phos. and periodic renal U/S for microcalcifications if on diuretics

Vitamin K (fat soluble)

Normal serum 30

Deficiency < 10

Is utilized in the prevention and treatment of neonatal hemorrhagic disease. Infants are born with decreased levels of clotting factors II, VII, IX, and X. These vitamin K dependent factors are decreased because of poor placental transfer of Vitamin K and lack of intestinal colonization with bacteria that synthesize Vitamin K. Three to five days after birth a rise in these clotting factors occurs; then a decline is seen. Vitamin K administration leads to near normal prothrombin times.

Excess of Vit K

*Hemolytic anemia

*Impaired binding of bilirubin to albumin

Deficiency of Vit K

*Necessary for manufacturing several clotting factors

(e.g., prothrombin and Factor VII).

*American Academy of Pediatrics recommends an IM dose: 0.5- 1.0mg IM at birth.

*Deficiency may result in hemorrhagic disease of the newborn. Infant may appear healthy, but will manifest bleeding from GI tract, umbilical cord, circ site, venipuncture sites, nose, as well as general ecchymosis.

*Hemorrhage as result Vit. Deficiency: Prophylactic Vit K may not have been given or mother may have been on coumadin or Dilantin

Transition to Enteral Feedings

  1. When enteral intake = 50 kcal/kg/day, decrease IV amino acids to1.5 gm/kg/day and D/C lipids.

  2. When enteral intake = 80 kcal/kg/day, D/C Hyperal and substitute IV dextrose for kcals (D10W then D5W).

  3. D/C IV when tolerating enteral feedings and gaining wt.

*Most LBW babies need approx. 110-130 kcal/kg/day to support normal growth and development however, some LBW babies may have higher energy needs relative to stress or energy expenditure of 140-150 kcal/kg/day

Initiation of Enteral Feedings

<1000g:

  • 1cc q 6hr x 24hr, then 1cc q 3hr x 24hr

  • Increase 10-20cc/day to >140cc/kg/day as tol.

1001-1500g:

  • Begin with 20cc/day as tol.

  • Increase 15cc-20cc/kg/day to >140cc/kg/day as tol.

1501-2000g:

  • Begin with 20cc/kg/day tol.

  • Increase 15cc to 20cc/kg/day to > 140cc/kg/day as tol.

>2000g:

  • 5cc q 3hr x 12hr

  • Increase 5cc q feed to 160-180cc/kg/day as tol.

  • Infants > 2000g will be able to PO feed on demand and set their own intake and feeding schedule

Withholding Feedings

  • Infant has just been extubated.

  • If residuals are more than 50% of last feeding.

  • Mucus residuals: green or large bright yellow residuals present more than once.

  • Abdomen is distended, may have bowel loops or discoloration.

  • Vomiting, blood is present in aspirate or stool.

  • Change in respiratory status: apnea and bradycardia, and TcPO2 drops along with distended abdomen or bile-stained residuals.

  • Some neonatologists recommend temporarily holding feeds when working up for rule out sepsis.

Anion Gap

  • Definition: The difference between the Na concentration and the sum of the chloride and bicarbonate concentrations (reflects the unaccounted for anion composition in the ECF). Na - (Cl + CO2).

  • Normal range: 5-15 mEq/L (15-22 preterm 9-12 term)

  • >15 = acidosis caused by accumulation of organic acids.

  • Poss. causes: renal failure, inborn errors of metabolism, lactic acidosis, late metabolic acidosis, and toxin exposure. (Lactic acidosis is most commonly d/t diminished tissue perfusion and resultant anaerobic metabolism in infants with asphyxia or severe cardiorespiratory disease.

  • <15 = acidosis due to loss of buffer by means of renal or GI system: renal tubular acidosis, acetazolamide, renal dysplasia, diarrhea, cholestyramine, small bowel drainage, dilutional acidosis, or hyperalimentation acidosis.

Retinopathy of Prematurity (ROP)

Vasoproliferative disorder that occurs in the developing retina of newborns, especially premature newborns < 1500 grams.

Three zones define the location of retinal involvement; extent of disease is shown as hours of the clock.

International Classification of Retinopathy of Prematurity

(ARCHIVES OF OPHALMOLOGY, VOL. 102: 1130-1134, AUGUST 1984)

Added note: ROP classification date

The classification cited here is from 1984. The International Classification of ROP has since been revised (2005, and ICROP3 in 2021).

STAGE 1

Demarcation line: This thin white line separates the avascular retina anteriorly from the vascularized retina posteriorly. Abnormal branching vessels lead to the demarcation line, which is flat and lies in the plane of the retina.

STAGE 2:

Ridge: The ridge has definite height and width, occupies a volume and extends up out of the plane of the retina. Color may change from white to pink. Small, isolated tufts of new vessels may appear posterior to the structure.

STAGE 3:

Ridge With Extraretinal Fibrovascular Proliferation: To the ridge of Stage 2 is added the presence of extraretinal fibrovascular proliferative tissue. Characteristic locations:

  • Continuous with the posterior aspect of the ridge (ragged ridge).

  • Immediately posterior to the ridge but not always appearing to be connected to it.

  • Into the vitreous perpendicular to the retinal.

STAGE 4:

Retinal Detachment: To Stage 3 is added unequivocal detachment of the retina caused by exudative effusion of fluid, traction, or both.

Plus Disease (+)

Progressive vascular incompetence is noted primarily by increasing dilatation and tortuosity of peripheral retinal vessels. Iris vascular engorgement, pupillary rigidity, and vitreous haze are also noted. Only when posterior veins are enlarged and arterioles are tortuous is the designation + added to the ROP Stage number, e.g., Stage 3 + ROP.

Possible Causes of ROP

MULTIFACTORIAL

  • Oxygen

  • Very low birthweight/prematurity

  • Those under 1,500 grams and especially those under 1,000 grams. (Low birthweight is the single most important factor in determining the risk for ROP.

  • Exchange Transfusion: Provides the newborn with an increased proportion of adult hemoglobin, which may result in a shift in the oxygen hemoglobin dissociation curve to the right, leading to retinal changes comparable to hyperoxia.

  • Intraventricular hemorrhage: With its associated shock, acidosis and hypoxia IVH may result in retinal hypoperfusion, which may cause vasoobliteration and damage

  • Apnea: Relationship between RLF and apnea when associated with bagging. It is unknown if the problem lies with hypoxia from apnea or by excessive use of oxygen during hand ventilation to treat apnea.

  • Sepsis: Direct endotoxic effect on the retinal tissue resulting from sepsis.

  • Increased or Decreased PCO2: Proposed that a PO2 level greater than 50% prevents retinal vasoconstriction and thereby exposes the retina to hyperoxic blood. Others believe that a decreased PCO2 level (below 25) plus resultant respiratory alkalosis (where the pH is greater than 7.55) leads to vasoconstriction decreased retinal blood flow, and ischemia.

  • Patent Ductus Arteriosis and Indomethacin: PDA causes shunting of blood between the pulmonary artery and the aorta with a difference between preductal and postductal PO2 values. This PO2 differential may affect retinal circulation. It has been questioned whether a drug a drug that causes constriction of a vessel such as the ductus arteriosus may have an effect upon the retinal vessel.

  • Vitamin E Deficiency: Is the most controversial issue in the treatment of RLF. Functions as a biologic antioxidant. The mechanism responsible for this effect is unknown.

FIRST OPHTHALMOLIGIST SCREEN AT SIX WEEKS.

* This screen includes all infants with the following criteria

  • Birthweight < 1500 grams

  • Larger infants with extended length of ventilator support or high oxygen therapy.

Ophthalmologist may want to continue to screen every one to two weeks, until vascularizaiton is complete.

PROGNOSIS:

  • Most of stage 1 or 2 will regress, although progression is unpredictable until vascularization is complete.

  • These stages are associated with a refractive error, amblyopia and strabismus.

  • In Stage 3 there is an incidence of refractive errors with strabismus requiring corrective lenses.

  • In 4A the macula is uninvolved. The prognosis of functional vision is good.

  • Poor prognosis secondary to the involvement of the macula.

Surgical Treatment

LASER THERAPY:

Alternative to cryotherapy since early 1990's Involves using a red light that is less invasive and less traumatic to the eye than cryotherapy and requires no anesthetic. It allows for deeper tissue penetration.

CRYOTHERAPY COMBINED WITH SCLERAL BUCKLING:

Involves encircling the globe of the eye with a band, which reduces the eyes diameter and increases the likelihood of retinal reattachment.

AFTER EFFECTS OF ROP (From NICU News May/June 1992)

  • Varying degrees of vision loss may result. If the retina detaches totally, the baby will be blind.

  • Other common effects: myopia (nearsightedness) or strabismus (a misalignment of eye muscles that causes the eye to turn inward or outward)

  • If either the brain or central nervous system is damaged or injured, it can affects infants vision.

  • IVH may injure the centers of the brain that control eye movement.

  • May cause strabismus (crossed eyes), blindness, or visual impressions of distance, speed, size of objects.

  • Optic Nerve Damage: May result from infection, lack of oxygen, trauma from birth, and IVH. When the fibers of the optic nerve are damaged, it may cause blindness or partial loss of sight.

Intraventricular Hemorrhage (IVH)

Classifications of IVH

GRADE I:

Subependymal hemorrhage in the periventricular germinal matrix. Often localized at the Foramen of Monro.

GRADE II:

Partial filling of lateral ventricles without ventricular dilation.

GRADE III:

Intraventricular hemorrhage with ventricular dilation.

GRADE IV:

Small and Large with parenchymal involvement or extension of blood into the cerebral tissue itself. Can be present to a lesser degree.

Clinical Presentation

  • Sudden deterioration

  • O2 Desaturation

  • Bradycardia

    • Metabolic Acidosis

  • Decrease in Hct.

  • Hypotonia

  • Shock

  • Hyperglycemia

  • Tense Anterior Fontanel

AS SYMPTOMS WORSEN:

Full tense fontanel

  • Increased Vent Support

  • Seizures

  • Apnea

  • Decreased level of consciousness or activity.

Subarachnoid Hemorrhage

Pathogenesis: usually result of normal trauma from birth. Source is normal bridging veins in subarachnoid, small leptomeningeal vessels or extension of cerebral contusion.

Features: neurologic signs of irritability or seizures.

Diagnosis: history of seizures and CSF findings. In between seizures neurological state normal. Confirmed with CT scan. Ultrasound not sensitive.

Management: Symptomatic therapy (i.e. anticonvulsant) attention to blood volume and cardiovascular status, treat hyperbilirubinemia, occasionally hydrocephalus develops in large bleeds.

Prognosis: vast majority do well with no sequelae.

Subdural Hemorrhage

Pathogenesis: result from trauma in full term infant; in preterm they may be a result from trauma or germinal matrix hemorrhage. Vertical molding, front-occipital elongation and torsional forces acting on the head may provoke laceration of dural leaflets of the tentorium cerebelli or falx cerebri

Presentation: accumulation of blood is rapid and large as arterioles large veins and sinuses have been ruptured. Presenting signs results from blood volume loss, neurologic dysfunction caused by increasing ICP or brain stem dysfunction. Seizures less common.

Signs and symptoms: bulging anterior fontanelle or increasing head circumference, lethargy, irritability, abnormal respirations, cranial nerve palsies, nystagmus, disconjugate gaze, hypotonia, vomiting, shock. Pallor, DIC, jaundice

Diagnosis: suspected on basis of clinical signs confirmed with CT

Management: attend to blood volume and cardiovascular status. Open surgical evacuation of clot if neurological signs present

Prognosis: normal development if surgery successful.

Screening of the Newborn

Phenylketonuria (PKU)

Is an inborn error of amino acid metabolism involving phenylalanine, which is inherited as an autosomal trait.

  • *Newborns with elevated blood phenylalanine level have PKU or a variant.

  • *Infants with galactosemia who are acutely ill may also have an increased concentration of phenylalanine.

PKU screening should be performed prior to discharge or 4th day.

With early nursery discharge, recent information indicates that most newborns with PKU have an elevated blood phenylalanine concentration during the first day of life.

Ventilator Weaning Strategies

General Concepts

  • If PaCO2 <50 and pH > 7.25 wean Amplitude

  • IF FiO2 < 70% with a PaO2 > 60 and good inflation on CXR wean MAP

  • Most babies have a fixed intrapulmonary shunt. In the absence of PPHN wean FiO2 aggressively

  • After 4-6 days of HFOV lung compliance should be assessed for possible DC of HFOV if equipment is needed

    • Low MAP

    • Doesn’t die with suctioning

    • Doesn’t need an ↑ in MAP after suctioning

  • As lung compliance normalizes many babies tolerate conventional ventilation

  • Extubation settings

    • Term: MAP 11-14; AMP up to 20

    • LBW: MAP 10-12; AMP up to 15

    • VLBW: Map <10; AMP <12

RDS and Term Newborns

  • If FiO2 < 70-80% wean MAP

  • Once well expanded generally based upon starting point can wean MAP by 3-4

  • Consider decreasing frequency in weaned MAP and No change in blood gas

  • If atelectasis occurs increase AMP

Air Leak / PIE

  • If FiO2 < 70-80% wean MAP aggressively to stop air leak

  • Fastest frequency possible

    • Delivers smallest volume

    • ↓frequency if air trapping

  • Decrease AMP and accept CO2 in high 50’s low 60’s

  • Position bad PIE lung down

Pneumothorax

  • If FiO2 < 70-80% wean MAP aggressively

  • Use lower frequency

  • As weaning of MAP occurs more AMP may be needed to avoid atelectasis

  • Accept higher CO2

  • Ensure good cardiac function and check for PPHN

  • Aggressively extubated to CPAP, start on Caffeine, do not over sedate so can be extubated

Uniform Hypoplasia

  • FiO2 depends on coexisting PPHN

  • Use the MAP that provides good lung inflation

    • Don’t count ribs look at lung fields clarity

  • Use 10—15 Hz but gelantous stuff in airways may air trap and Hz may need to be decreased

  • Use smallest possible AMP

  • Maintain HFV for as long as possible

Non-Uniform Hypoplasia

  • FiO2 depends on co-existing PPHN

  • For appropriate MAP look at normal lung expansion

    • Asses preductal gases from an R radial art line for line in L atrium

  • Lower Hz

  • Maintain normal CO2 with minimal AMP changes

  • Ensure good cardiac function
