Removing a UAC

Steps Atlas

  1. Leave the umbilical tie loose around the stump.
  2. Withdraw slowly until about 5 cm remain, then tighten the tie.
  3. Stop the infusion.
  4. Pull the rest at 1 cm per minute to allow vasospasm.
  5. For bleeding, pinch the cord from the sides.

Reminders for every umbilical line

  • Add the stump length to every formula.
  • Confirm the tip on film before using the line.
  • A formula is a starting point; the film decides.

Needle decompression

Emergency only, for a critically compromised baby. It buys time while you set up the chest tube.

Site Atlas

2nd intercostal space, midclavicular line, or 4th intercostal space, anterior axillary line. Avoid the breast bud. The Atlas prefers the anterior approach so the lateral chest stays clear for the tube.

Equipment Atlas

20- or 22-gauge angiocath, T-connector, 3-way stopcock, 10 and 20 mL syringes.

Technique Atlas

  1. Pull the skin sideways to make a Z-track.
  2. Insert perpendicular, just over the top of the lower rib.
  3. Advance slowly until you lose resistance, see humidity, or hear air. Slide the catheter in and remove the needle.
  4. Attach the T-connector and stopcock and evacuate with the syringe rather than leaving it open to air.

If a tube will follow, remove air only until vital signs stabilize. The leftover air pocket makes tube insertion safer.

For a nontension pneumothorax, especially in a baby not on positive pressure, needle evacuation is sometimes all that's needed.

Source: MacDonald's Atlas, ch. 41, Thoracostomy.

5 mL/kg per 2–4 minute cycle. Draw and infuse slowly, at least a minute each, to avoid swings in blood pressure and intracranial pressure.

Method Atlas

Push-pull: through a UVC; single lumen preferred, since double lumen adds resistance.
Isovolumetric: donor blood in through the UVC or a PIV, baby's blood out through a UAC, UVC, or peripheral arterial line. Better tolerated in sick or unstable babies.

Blood Atlas

As fresh as possible (<7 days), irradiated within 24 hours of use, through a thermostatic blood warmer. Hct 40–45% is preferred for hyperbilirubinemia.

Before: draw any diagnostic tests first (serologies, metabolic screen, genetics), plus Hb/Hct, platelets, electrolytes, calcium, gas, glucose, total and direct bilirubin, and coags. NPO 4 hours if possible; empty the stomach; start a PIV for glucose.

During: glucose every 60 minutes; gases as needed.

After: glucose every 2–4 hours for 24 hours. Ionized calcium and platelets right away in sick or preterm babies. Hb, Hct, and bilirubin about 4 hours later.

Peripheral arterial line

Sites Atlas

Radial is most common, then ulnar and posterior tibial; dorsalis pedis occasionally. Avoid axillary and brachial (poor collateral flow) and temporal (neurologic risk). Use the right arm when you need preductal values.

An umbilical arterial line is usually preferred in the newborn period.

Collateral check Atlas

Do an Allen test before a radial line, but it's not fully reliable, so keep watching perfusion during and after. For the foot, occlude dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial, release one, and look for reperfusion within 10 seconds. Don't hyperextend the wrist; it can occlude the artery and give a false result.

Catheter Atlas

24G (0.56 or 0.75 inch) or 22G × 1 inch. Transilluminate the wrist in 45–60° extension. Flush gently with 0.5 mL and watch for blanching.

Use it for sampling and pressure only; nothing but the heparinized flush runs through it.

Decision tree

  1. Bed loose (warm, low diastolic, wide pulse pressure) → norepinephrine (or dopamine)
  2. Pump weak (poor contractility on echo, cool) → dobutamine
  3. Pump weak and afterload high (high SVR, PPHN, post-op) → milrinone; add a vasopressor if BP sags
  4. Both failing, unstable → epinephrine
  5. Still low on catecholamines → vasopressin; think hydrocortisone

Using it

  • High BP: ≥95th percentile on 3 separate occasions. Above the 95th, watch closely; above the 99th, investigate and consider treating.
  • First days of life: BP rises on its own with postnatal age, birth weight, and GA. A BP that fails to rise, or falls, needs a look. Readings below the 10th or above the 90th percentile are common in babies who look well.
  • "MAP ≥ GA in weeks": the review calls this rule not physiologically based, with little evidence and no link to better outcomes. Judge BP with perfusion and the whole picture.
  • Measuring: an arterial line is the gold standard. A cuff measures the MAP and calculates systolic and diastolic, so compare MAP. Use the right arm; cuff bladder about 40% of arm circumference wide, covering 80–100% of it.

Treatment algorithm

  1. Is perfusion adequate? If yes, permissive hypotension. Check NIRS, urine output, lactate, and echo, not just the cuff.
  2. Identify the phenotype. Low SVR (warm, wide pulse pressure, low diastolic) → norepinephrine or dopamine. Low output (cool, narrow pulse pressure) → dobutamine. Mixed → epinephrine if output is low, norepinephrine if SVR is low.
  3. If refractory, add by problem. Norepinephrine for vasoplegia; epinephrine for poor contractility; vasopressin if catecholamine-resistant; milrinone for low output with pulmonary hypertension.
  4. Still refractory: hydrocortisone.
  5. Reassess with echo, NIRS, urine output, and lactate; watch for too much vasoconstriction (cool extremities, rising lactate).
  6. Wean catecholamines first, keep milrinone while pulmonary hypertension persists, and wean vasopressin last in vasoplegia.

If BP improves but perfusion worsens, you may be over-constricting.

Read a blood gas

Four steps

  1. pH: below 7.35 acidemic, above 7.45 alkalemic.
  2. PaCO₂: respiratory? It's the real-time ventilation marker.
  3. HCO₃⁻ and base excess: metabolic?
  4. Compensation, then the bedside: does it fit the work of breathing, oxygen need, and perfusion?

CO₂ moves opposite pH → respiratory. HCO₃⁻ moves with pH → metabolic.

Apnea of prematurity

Apnea: no breathing for ≥20 seconds, or a shorter pause with bradycardia (<100), cyanosis, or pallor. Most events in preterm infants are mixed (central plus obstructive).

Periodic breathing: cycles of breathing 10–18 seconds long broken by pauses of at least 3 seconds, repeating for at least 2 minutes. Normal.

Caffeine citrate

Load 20 mg/kg, then 5 mg/kg once daily; up to 10 mg/kg/day if apnea continues. Routine levels aren't needed.

Trial off after 5–7 days free of significant apnea off positive pressure, or at 33–34 weeks PMA, whichever comes first. Keep cardiorespiratory monitoring for 7 days after stopping (caffeine's half-life is 50–100 hours).

Getting ready for discharge

Most preterm infants are stable by 36–37 weeks PMA, and about 80% are free of apnea and bradycardia by the time they're otherwise ready. Very preterm or complicated infants can take until 43–44 weeks.

Don't need to delay discharge: brief, isolated bradycardias that resolve on their own; feeding-related events that resolve when the feed is paused.

Home apnea monitors are rarely indicated: they don't prevent SIDS and shouldn't be used to send home an infant whose breathing control isn't stable. There's no evidence that reflux causes apnea, and reflux medicines aren't recommended for it.

Apnea can briefly increase after surgery, eye exams, and 2-month vaccines in very preterm infants.

Hyperglycemia

Definition: serum glucose >150 mg/dL (whole blood >125). Persistent levels over 200 in VLBW infants need a careful look.

Causes: too much glucose, prematurity, stress hormones, sepsis, TPN, delayed feeds, steroids and other medications. Glucosuria of 1+ or more means the renal threshold is passed: risk of osmotic diuresis, dehydration, and electrolyte losses.

Treat

  1. Lower the GIR (dextrose % or rate) by 1–2 mg/kg/min about every 2 hours, checking glucose often, down to 4 mg/kg/min.
  2. Insulin if glucose stays above 250 with urine glucose 2+ or more on two samples 4 hours apart despite the GIR cut: infusion starting 0.01–0.05 units/kg/hr, titrated by 0.01, max 0.1. Target 100–150. Halve it at 180 and stop it below 150.

Insulin boluses carry a high hypoglycemia risk and are generally avoided. Check glucose often on insulin; responses are unpredictable.

In stable preterm infants, increase by 18–30 mL/kg/day. After day 4, advancing at 30 mL/kg/day didn't raise NEC or death compared with 15–20 (SIFT, about 2,800 infants) and reached full feeds sooner.

Warning signs

Abdominal distension or discoloration, visible loops, bilious or bloody aspirates or vomiting, blood in the stool, and new apnea and bradycardia with a distended abdomen.

Sodium problems

HypernatremiaNa >150 (osmolality >300)
HyponatremiaNa <130. Below 120, seizures are common: an emergency.
GoalKeep Na 132–145.

Low Na in the first few days is usually dilutional (excess free water). High Na is usually dehydration from insensible water loss or osmotic diuresis, or too much sodium, unless there's renal failure. A rise of 10 mEq/L in 24 hours points to dehydration or sodium overload.

High Na from water loss: raise water 10–20 mL/kg/day. Once Na normalizes (3–5 days), add sodium, titrating 1–2 mEq/kg/day of NaCl, or Na acetate if acidotic.

Electrolytes every 6–12 hours the first day until stable, then daily the first week, then every other day to weekly.

Tachycardia is an early sign of dehydration; hypotension is late.

Urine Na loss (mEq/kg/day) = urine Na (mEq/L) ÷ 1000 × urine output (mL/day) ÷ kg

Potassium problems

Hyperkalemia

K >6 mmol/L in a non-hemolyzed sample. Heel sticks hemolyze, so confirm with a venous or arterial sample, but never delay treatment in a symptomatic baby.

Causes: too much potassium (fluids, older transfused blood); shift out of cells (acidosis: each 0.1 drop in pH raises K about 0.3–1.3; hyperosmolality; tissue breakdown such as NEC, hemolysis, IVH); poor renal excretion (kidney injury, CAH). Tiny preterm infants get non-oliguric hyperkalemia from immature tubules.

K 6–7Stop all potassium. STAT 12-lead ECG. ECG changes: treat. No changes: watch and trend.
K ≥7Stop all potassium, STAT ECG, and treat even if the ECG is normal.

ECG progression: peaked T → flat P, longer PR → wide QRS → SVT/VT, bradycardia, VF.

Treat

Protect the heart10% calcium gluconate 100 mg/kg IV over 10–30 min. Lasts 30–60 min; repeat as needed. Doesn't lower K.
Shift K into cellsInsulin 0.05 units/kg with D10 2 mL/kg, then insulin 0.05–0.1 units/kg/hr with D10; watch glucose closely.
Albuterol neb 0.4 mg/kg once: fast, lasts about 2 hours; tachycardia.
Sodium bicarbonate 4.2% 1–2 mEq/kg over 30–60 min; give slowly if <34 wk and <3 days old (IVH risk). Not in the same line as calcium (it precipitates).
Remove KFurosemide 1 mg/kg IV. Rectal Kayexalate 1 g/kg is not recommended in preterm infants or with compromised bowel. Dialysis or exchange as a last resort.

Hypokalemia (K <3.5)

Causes: too little in fluids; losses (thiazides, amphotericin B, severe diarrhea); alkalosis (each 0.1 rise in pH lowers K about 0.3–1.3).

Signs: ileus, weakness, decreased reflexes. ECG: long QT, flat T, ST depression.

In tiny preterm infants whose K started high, start potassium in the IV fluids as it falls toward 4 (usually days 3–6).

Sources: UNC NCCC Management of Hyperkalemia guideline (2019); CAHS WA Neonatology, Hyperkalaemia; NNP Brain (program reference).

Calcium problems

Hypocalcemia

Total Ca <8 mg/dL in term, <7 in preterm infants (first 3 days); ionized Ca <1.0 mmol/L. Ionized calcium is the biologically active form.

Ca falls for 12–24 hours after birth, rises after 72 hours, and is normal by 5–10 days. Early hypocalcemia is common after asphyxia and in IDMs.

Treat (Ca <6 mg/dL): 10% calcium gluconate 100–200 mg/kg/dose over 30 minutes (push only in an emergency). Stop if HR drops below 100. Maintenance during treatment up to 200–800 mg/kg/day. Correct low magnesium too.

Hypercalcemia (Ca >11)

Causes: neonatal hyperparathyroidism (primary, or from maternal hypoparathyroidism), phosphate depletion (low-phosphate human milk), abnormal vitamin D metabolism, too much calcium infusion.

Signs: hypotonia, weakness, irritability, poor feeding, hypertension, weight loss, vomiting, constipation, polyuria and dehydration, fat necrosis. ECG: short QT, arrhythmias.

Manage: furosemide (calciuretic), restrict calcium, add phosphate if needed, calcitonin to oppose PTH.

Complications: nephrocalcinosis, renal failure, RTA, metastatic calcification, hypertensive encephalopathy.

Hypercalciuria: spot urine Ca:creatinine >0.21–0.25, or 24-hour urine Ca >4 mg/kg.

Source: NNP Brain (program reference).

Magnesium

Normal 1.5–2.8 mg/dL. Levels at birth track gestational age (lower when less mature).

Low Mg (<1.5) with low Ca: correct the magnesium; the calcium won't normalize until you do.

High Mg (often after prolonged maternal MgSO₄; levels around 9–12 mg/dL): decreased reflexes, respiratory depression, ECG changes.

Infants under 1.2 kg may need extra electrolytes; follow calcium, phosphorus, and alkaline phosphatase for bone mineralization.

Neurotoxicity risk factors AAP 2022

Gestational age <38 weeks; albumin <3.0 g/dL; isoimmune hemolytic disease, G6PD deficiency, or other hemolysis; sepsis; significant clinical instability in the past 24 hours. Any of these lowers the thresholds.

Screening

Look at every newborn for jaundice at least every 12 hours. Jaundice before 24 hours: TcB or TSB now. Otherwise check at 24–48 hours or before discharge.

Get a TSB if the TcB is above the phototherapy threshold, within 3 mg/dL of it, or ≥15. Treatment decisions use the TSB; don't subtract the direct. If direct is more than 50% of the TSB, call an expert.

A rise of ≥0.3 mg/dL/hr in the first 24 hours suggests hemolysis: get a DAT. After 24 hours, ≥0.2 mg/dL/hr counts as a rapid rise. A TSB at the phototherapy line before 24 hours also points to hemolysis.

Phototherapy

Intensive = narrow-spectrum blue LED, ≥30 µW/cm²/nm at 460–490 nm. A bili blanket is not intensive. Keep feeds going.

Stop when the TSB is at least 2 mg/dL below the threshold where you started. Rebound TSB about 24 hours later, or at 6–12 hours if phototherapy started before 48 hours of age.

Escalation of care AAP 2022

TSB within 2 mg/dL of the exchange threshold = emergency

Manage in the NICU with intensive phototherapy and PO plus IV hydration. STAT: total and direct bilirubin, CBC, albumin, chemistry, type and screen, ABO/Rh, DAT, reticulocyte count. TSB every 2 hours until below the escalation level.

Exchange

TSB at or above the exchange threshold: urgent exchange. If the TSB falls below it while you prepare and there are no signs of intermediate or advanced acute bilirubin encephalopathy, you may hold the exchange, continue intensive phototherapy, and check every 2 hours.

Prolonged jaundice

Beyond 3–4 weeks breastfed or 2 weeks formula-fed: total and direct bilirubin and the newborn screen.

If done: replace with an equal volume of normal saline, as early as possible (viscosity peaks 2–4 hours after birth), using 80 mL/kg as blood volume. Watch glucose: if IV fluids are needed, dextrose at ≥100 mL/kg/day (GIR 6–8) for the first 24–48 hours.

Baylor Guidelines for Acute Care of the Neonate, Ed. 34, Ch. 8.6.

Isoimmunization

Fetal red cells enter the mother's circulation and trigger antibodies. Maternal IgG crosses back, binds the baby's red cells, and destroys them.

Rh disease

An Rh-negative mother is sensitized by an Rh-positive fetus; disease is worse in later pregnancies.

ABO disease

Mother type O, baby A or B. The most common isoimmune hemolytic disease, usually mild and managed with phototherapy. Only IgG anti-A or anti-B crosses the placenta, and disease occurs when the mother's IgG titers are high.

Findings: jaundice within 24 hours, mild or no anemia, DAT negative or weakly positive, spherocytes, raised reticulocyte count.

Source: NNP Brain (program reference).

Subarachnoid hemorrhage

Usually from ordinary birth trauma (bridging veins, small leptomeningeal vessels). Irritability or seizures, with a normal exam between seizures. Confirm with CT; ultrasound isn't sensitive.

Manage: treat seizures, support blood volume and circulation, treat hyperbilirubinemia. Large bleeds occasionally cause hydrocephalus.

Most do well with no long-term effects.

Source: NNP Brain (program reference).

Subdural hemorrhage

Usually trauma in term infants (molding and twisting forces tear the tentorium or falx); in preterm infants, trauma or germinal matrix bleeding. Bleeding is rapid and large.

Signs: bulging fontanelle or growing head circumference, lethargy, irritability, abnormal breathing, cranial nerve palsies, nystagmus, disconjugate gaze, hypotonia, vomiting, shock, pallor, DIC, jaundice. Seizures are less common.

Diagnose with CT. Manage: support blood volume and circulation; surgical evacuation if there are neurologic signs.

Source: NNP Brain (program reference).

Adrenal crisis

Triggers: infection, injury, physical stress.

Signs: vomiting, extreme weakness, dehydration, hypotension, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia, fever.

Treat now

Fluids, then hydrocortisone.

Birth to 6 weeks25 mg IV or IM, then 5–10 mg every 6 hours
By surface area50–100 mg/m² bolus, then 50–100 mg/m²/day divided every 6 hours

Use the higher end if the baby is hypovolemic, hyponatremic, or hyperkalemic; hydrocortisone's mineralocorticoid effect helps.

After

Once stable, step down to oral stress dosing (about 30–50 mg/m²/day), then maintenance (about 10–15 mg/m²/day in CAH, in 2–3 doses). Primary adrenal insufficiency also needs fludrocortisone, set with endocrinology.

Diagnosis: cortisol, then a cosyntropin (ACTH) stimulation test, interpreted with endocrinology.

Sources: Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne, Adrenal crisis guideline; Endotext, Adrenal Insufficiency in Children; Medscape, Pediatric Adrenal Insufficiency; NNP Brain (signs).

Seizures

Treat the seizure, then find the cause. Correct low glucose, calcium, or sodium first. Base drug decisions on EEG-confirmed seizures when you can.

Medications

First linePhenobarbital 20 mg/kg IV. Second load 10–20 mg/kg if needed. Watch for respiratory depression and hypotension.
Second lineLevetiracetam 40 mg/kg IV (second load 20 mg/kg; maintenance 40–60 mg/kg/day in 3 doses), preferred with heart disease.
Or fosphenytoin 20 mg/kg IV (phenytoin equivalents).
Other optionsMidazolam 0.1 mg/kg IV, then 0.1 mg/kg/hr.
Lidocaine 2 mg/kg over 10 min, then 7 mg/kg/hr for 4 h, 3.5 for 12 h, 1.75 for 12 h, stop; adjust for weight, PMA, and cooling.

Phenobarbital controlled seizures in 80% vs 28% with levetiracetam in the head-to-head trial. If a channelopathy is suspected (family history), a sodium channel blocker (phenytoin or carbamazepine) goes first.

Workup

  • Glucose; Na, K, Cl, CO₂, Ca, Mg
  • BUN and creatinine; AST, ALT, ammonia, lactate
  • PT, PTT, fibrinogen, D-dimer
  • Sepsis workup, bacterial and viral
  • ECG and echo; cranial ultrasound; chest film
  • Ongoing seizures: urine amino and organic acids

HIE and cooling

Who gets cooled: all three

1. Age and GA≥36 weeks, and cooling can start within 6 hours of birth
2. Acidosis or eventCord or first-hour gas: pH ≤7.0 or base deficit ≥16.
If pH 7.01–7.15, BD 10–15.9, or no gas: an acute perinatal event (abruption, cord prolapse, severe FHR abnormality) and a 10-minute Apgar ≤5 or ventilation (including CPAP) started at birth and still needed at 10 minutes
3. EncephalopathyModerate or severe: seizures, or moderate/severe signs in at least 3 of the 6 modified Sarnat categories (level of consciousness, spontaneous activity, posture, tone, primitive reflexes, autonomic)

Usually excluded: known chromosomal anomaly, major congenital anomalies, severe IUGR (<1800 g).

Cooling

Esophageal temperature 33.5 °C for 72 hours on a servo-controlled blanket, then rewarm slowly by 0.5 °C per hour.

While cooled

Continuous EEG and neurology; treat seizures (see Seizures). Blood culture and ampicillin plus an aminoglycoside. NPO with TPN at the start; trophic feeds may begin at 12–24 hours if stable. Morphine for shivering or discomfort. Watch glucose, coagulation, and blood pressure; avoid bicarbonate if possible. Turn every 2 hours to protect the skin.

Sources: NICHD whole-body hypothermia trial (Shankaran et al., NEJM 2005) for eligibility and cooling; Baylor Guidelines Ed. 34, Ch. 12.2–12.3 for exclusions and care during cooling.

Baylor Baylor practice

Same core criteria. Baylor may also cool, after a documented shared decision with parents: infants 35 0/7–35 6/7 weeks, or ≥36 weeks who are 6–24 hours old.

Mild HIE isn't cooled routinely. Exam hourly until the 6-hour window closes. First modified Sarnat exam at about 1 hour of life, by two NICU providers.

Referral hospital: start passive cooling once the baby qualifies (remove all heat sources) and check temperature every 15 minutes to avoid overcooling.

Esophageal probe: nose to earlobe to xiphoid, minus 2 cm; confirm on 2-view x-ray with the tip at T7–T9.

Thyroid

Newborn screen (Texas)

The state lab measures T4 first; the lowest 10% are retested with TSH. A positive screen isn't a diagnosis: confirm with a venous free T4 and TSH, and call pediatric endocrinology.

High TSH, low FT4Primary hypothyroidism: start treatment promptly
High TSH, normal FT4Subclinical: treat if TSH is markedly high (typically >20 mIU/L); otherwise watch and retest
Low or normal TSH, low FT4Central hypothyroidism, but also common in preterm or sick infants; repeat before treating and look for other pituitary deficiencies

Levothyroxine: usually 10–15 mcg/kg/day to start, individualized. Crushed tablet in a little water or breast milk, same time daily, apart from soy, calcium, and iron.

Clues: often none at birth. Goiter, prolonged indirect hyperbilirubinemia, a wide posterior fontanelle; later lethargy, poor feeding, constipation, hypotonia, hypothermia, dry skin, coarse face, hoarse cry.

Preterm hypothyroxinemia

Low T4 with normal or low TSH is common under 32 weeks and usually resolves in 4–8 weeks. Treating it hasn't been shown to help (Cochrane). VLBW infants also have an 8-fold higher risk of transient primary hypothyroidism (low T4, high TSH).

Thyroid levels change quickly after birth; use your lab's age-specific ranges.

IV infiltration

  1. Stop the infusion and disconnect the tubing. Don't flush.
  2. Leave the catheter in only if you'll aspirate or give an antidote through it; otherwise remove it.
  3. Elevate the limb (or the head of the bed for a scalp site). Use dry cold or warm compresses only if the specific drug's guidance calls for them. Don't apply heat, especially moist heat, to an IV fluid infiltration.
  4. Tell the provider if the site stays swollen, red, blanched, or dark. Consider plastic surgery.

Normal vital signs

Temperature36.5–37.5 °C axillary. Rectal temps don't reflect core temperature and can injure the newborn.
Heart rate120–160. Tachycardia >180. Bradycardia <80. At birth, a HR below 100 calls for PPV.
Respiratory rate40–60. Tachypnea >60.
ApneaNo breathing for ≥20 seconds, or shorter with bradycardia (<100), cyanosis, or pallor.
Periodic breathingBreathing cycles of 10–18 seconds broken by pauses of at least 3 seconds, repeating for at least 2 minutes. Normal.
Blood pressureSee BP norms in the CV tab.

Central venous pressure

Normal 3–7 mmHg. Bringing CVP to 5–8 mmHg with volume is associated with better cardiac output; above 5–8, more volume usually doesn't help. Ventilator pressures and tricuspid valve function also change CVP.

Sources: NRP (temperature, HR at birth); Baylor College of Medicine, Guidelines for Acute Care of the Neonate, Ed. 34 (2026–2027), Ch. 16.1 (apnea, periodic breathing); NNP Brain (program reference).

Newborn screening (Texas)

Blood spot screen

Texas screens for more than 50 disorders, including cystic fibrosis, SCID, SMA, inborn errors of metabolism, congenital hypothyroidism, galactosemia, hemoglobinopathies, CAH, and biotinidase deficiency. Pompe, MPS I, MPS II, and infantile Krabbe were added in 2025; GAMT deficiency in June 2026.

First screen24–48 hours of age, on every newborn regardless of feeding or prematurity
Second screen1–2 weeks of age
TransfusionCollect the first screen before any transfusion (including ECMO or emergency blood). After a transfusion, retest 6–8 weeks later.

Other routine screens

Bilirubin: total and direct at 24–48 hours, with the blood spot. A high direct (conjugated) bilirubin can be an early sign of biliary atresia.

Glucose: LGA, SGA, preterm (<37 wk), and IDM infants: first check at 30 minutes to 2 hours of life, then regularly for 12–24 hours. Many nurseries use 40% oral glucose gel with a feed as first treatment. See the Hypoglycemia card (FEN).

CCHD and hearing: see their cards below.

Source: Baylor College of Medicine, Guidelines for Acute Care of the Neonate, Ed. 34 (2026–2027), Ch. 13.11.

CCHD pulse ox screen

Required in Texas. Infant off oxygen, at 24 hours or older (or before discharge). Check the right hand and one foot.

Either site <90%Fail. Don't repeat; get an immediate clinical assessment.
Both ≥95% and difference ≤3%Pass. Routine care.
Anything elseRetest once in 1 hour. Then both ≥95% and difference ≤3% passes; anything else fails.

The 2025 AAP algorithm allows one retest. Confirmed CCHD must be reported to Texas DSHS. Screening also picks up non-cardiac causes of low saturation: infection, lung disease, PPHN, hemoglobinopathy, hypothermia.

Sources: AAP Clinical Report, Newborn Screening for CCHD: A New Algorithm (Oster et al., Pediatrics 2025); Baylor College of Medicine, Guidelines for Acute Care of the Neonate, Ed. 34 (2026–2027), Ch. 13.11.

Hearing screen

Screen by 1 month, diagnose by 3 months, intervene by 6 months

Required by law in Texas. Hearing loss affects about 1–3 per 1,000 well newborns but 20–40 per 1,000 NICU infants, and risk factors alone catch only about half.

Well babiesOAE or AABR. If referred, rescreen before discharge. If the rescreen refers on either side: urine CMV PCR and an outpatient hearing appointment.
NICUAABR required. If referred on either side: diagnostic audiology evaluation (ABR) plus urine or saliva CMV PCR; ENT referral if hearing loss is confirmed.

NICU timing

Can screen: >34 weeks PMA, medically stable; in a crib, isolette, or warmer; on CPAP, nasal cannula, or a ventilator.

Wait if: <34 weeks PMA, on HFOV, on continuous EEG, or on vasoactive drips.

Rescreen infants readmitted in the first month with hyperbilirubinemia needing exchange or culture-positive sepsis or meningitis.

Source: Baylor College of Medicine, Guidelines for Acute Care of the Neonate, Ed. 34 (2026–2027), Ch. 13.11 and 2.1.

Discharge readiness

Every newborn

  • Exam and course show nothing needing continued hospital care
  • Stable vital signs for 12 hours, including temperature in an open crib
  • Two successful feeds in a row (latch, swallow, and satiety documented, or suck-swallow-breathe coordination for bottle feeds)
  • Has voided and stooled at least once
  • Sepsis risk managed per current guidelines
  • Maternal HIV, syphilis, hepatitis B surface antigen, and GBS results reviewed
  • Blood spot, hearing, and CCHD screens done
  • Bilirubin risk assessed and follow-up arranged

Preterm and NICU infants, also

  • Car seat observation for apnea, bradycardia, or desaturation
  • CPR training offered to parents
  • Hearing screen done; ROP follow-up exam scheduled if the retina isn't mature or ROP was treated
  • High-risk developmental follow-up (all <1500 g birth weight)
  • RSV prevention (nirsevimab) as appropriate
  • Apnea criteria met: see Apnea of prematurity (Resp)

Source: Baylor College of Medicine, Guidelines for Acute Care of the Neonate, Ed. 34 (2026–2027), Ch. 13.9 and 2.1 (citing the AAP Committee on Fetus and Newborn).

How to read an x-ray

Remember RIPE ABCDEFG.

R: RotationClavicle equal from spine
I: InspirationT9 expansion. Divide in 3 zones; look at each zone for symmetry, vascular markings, air in lung fields or outside. Fluid or blood causes increased opacity.
P: ProjectionAP, left lateral, cross table
E: ExposureLeft hemidiaphragm visible to spine. Vertebrae visible behind heart.
A: AirwayTrachea and bronchus: size, shape, shadow, site. Pushing trachea: pleural effusion or tension pneumothorax. Pulling trachea: consolidation with lobar collapse.
B: BonesFractures or change in bone density? Extra or missing? Lateral deviations?
C: Circulation / CardiacSite: left or right. Size: less than 1/2 the transthoracic diameter (is the largest diameter of the heart less than 1/2 the largest diameter of the thorax?). Shape: apex pointing to the left? Shadows: any change in density. Borders: clear or well defined?
D: DiaphragmOutline should be clear and smooth. Subdiaphragmatic air? (pneumoperitoneum)
E: EquipmentKnow your lines (below)
F: Featureless featuresLoss of normal markings in chest or abdomen. Too much air, too little, or no air (physiologic in origin).
G: Gut and gasesStomach: left upper quadrant, variable amount of air. Small bowel: intraluminal gas usually minimal within multiple tight loops. Large bowel: mix of gas and stool. Any fixed loops? Abnormally large gas areas?
