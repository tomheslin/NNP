Needle decompression

Emergency only, for a critically compromised baby. It buys time while you set up the chest tube.

Site Atlas

2nd intercostal space, midclavicular line, or 4th intercostal space, anterior axillary line. Avoid the breast bud. The Atlas prefers the anterior approach so the lateral chest stays clear for the tube.

Equipment Atlas

20- or 22-gauge angiocath, T-connector, 3-way stopcock, 10 and 20 mL syringes.

Differs: the Atlas says a catheter-over-needle is less traumatic than straight needles or butterfly (scalp vein) sets. Other guidelines use a 23–25G butterfly (Safer Care Victoria) or 18–24G angiocath (Cochrane review).

Technique Atlas

  1. Pull the skin sideways to make a Z-track.
  2. Insert perpendicular, just over the top of the lower rib.
  3. Advance slowly until you lose resistance, see humidity, or hear air. Slide the catheter in and remove the needle.
  4. Attach the T-connector and stopcock and evacuate with the syringe rather than leaving it open to air.

If a tube will follow, remove air only until vital signs stabilize. The leftover air pocket makes tube insertion safer.

For a nontension pneumothorax, especially in a baby not on positive pressure, needle evacuation is sometimes all that's needed.

Sources: Atlas ch. 41, Thoracostomy; Safer Care Victoria, Pneumothorax drainage for neonates (2014); Cochrane review CD011724 (2019).

Chest tube

Site Atlas

4th (or 5th) intercostal space, just anterior to the midaxillary line. A horizontal line from the nipple marks about the 4th space. Enter over the top of the rib.

Size

Pigtail Atlas6 or 8 Fr if <28 wk
8 Fr if >28 wk
Straight tube Atlas8–12 Fr

Differs: Safer Care Victoria uses a pigtail of 8 Fr under 1500 g and 10 Fr over 1500 g.

Technique Atlas

Pigtails go in percutaneously. For a straight tube, a trocar is not recommended because of the risk of lung perforation; use blunt dissection and puncture the pleura with closed forceps.

For air, direct the tip cephalad and anterior, toward the apex (midclavicle), with all side holes inside the chest.

Suction Atlas

10–15 cm H₂O for most ventilated infants. Too much suction can pull tissue into the side holes.

Confirm Atlas

AP and lateral films, both, to confirm the anterior course and that the side holes are in the chest. Bubbling pattern and ultrasound help.

The line between the iliac crests is usually said to cross L4 or L4–L5, but in neonates it can cross as low as L5. Palpate carefully.

Position Atlas

Sitting with hips flexed gives the widest spaces; lateral decubitus with hips flexed is second. Don't flex the neck: it doesn't widen the spaces and raises the risk of airway obstruction. Lying on the side with knees flexed can cause brief desaturation, so watch the monitor.

Needle Atlas

Bevelled spinal needle with stylet: 25G × 1 inch or 22G × 1.5 inch. Always have the stylet in to pass the skin (epidermoid tumor risk).

Midline, angled slightly cephalad toward the umbilicus. Once past the skin, either take the stylet out and advance slowly, or leave it in and check often. Stylet-out has a higher first-try success, but whether it raises the epidermoid risk is unclear. Replace the stylet before removing the needle.

Don't do it if Atlas

Signs of raised intracranial pressure (image first), uncorrected thrombocytopenia or bleeding disorder, skin infection at the site, lumbosacral anomalies, or instability where the risk outweighs the benefit.

Exchange transfusion

Blood volume Atlas

Term about 80–85 mL/kg. Preterm about 100–120 mL/kg.

Other guidelines use term 80–90 and preterm 90–100 mL/kg.

How much Atlas

Double volume2 × blood volume. Exchanges about 85% of the blood volume.
Single volume1 × blood volume. Exchanges about 60%.
Partial, severe anemiaBlood volume × (Hb desired − Hb initial) ÷ (Hb of PRBC − Hb initial)
Partial, polycythemiaBlood volume × desired Hct change ÷ initial Hct

Order an extra 25–30 mL for the tubing and blood warmer.

Rate Atlas

5 mL/kg per 2–4 minute cycle. Draw and infuse slowly, at least a minute each, to avoid swings in blood pressure and intracranial pressure.

Method Atlas

Push-pull: through a UVC; single lumen preferred, since double lumen adds resistance.
Isovolumetric: donor blood in through the UVC or a PIV, baby's blood out through a UAC, UVC, or peripheral arterial line. Better tolerated in sick or unstable babies.

Blood Atlas

As fresh as possible (<7 days), irradiated within 24 hours of use, through a thermostatic blood warmer. Hct 40–45% is preferred for hyperbilirubinemia.

Before, during, after Atlas

Before: draw any diagnostic tests first (serologies, metabolic screen, genetics), plus Hb/Hct, platelets, electrolytes, calcium, gas, glucose, total and direct bilirubin, and coags. NPO 4 hours if possible; empty the stomach; start a PIV for glucose.

During: glucose every 60 minutes; gases as needed.

After: glucose every 2–4 hours for 24 hours. Ionized calcium and platelets right away in sick or preterm babies. Hb, Hct, and bilirubin about 4 hours later.

Sources: Atlas ch. 49, Exchange Transfusions; Brigham and Women's Newborn Medicine guideline; Italian recommendations for transfusion therapy in neonatology (PMC4607607).

Peripheral arterial line

Sites Atlas

Radial is most common, then ulnar and posterior tibial; dorsalis pedis occasionally. Avoid axillary and brachial (poor collateral flow) and temporal (neurologic risk). Use the right arm when you need preductal values.

One NICU guideline (Leeds) calls the umbilical arterial line the arterial line of choice in the newborn.

Collateral check Atlas

Do an Allen test before a radial line, but it's not fully reliable, so keep watching perfusion during and after. For the foot, occlude dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial, release one, and look for reperfusion within 10 seconds. Don't hyperextend the wrist; it can occlude the artery and give a false result.

Catheter Atlas

24G (0.56 or 0.75 inch) or 22G × 1 inch. Transilluminate the wrist in 45–60° extension. Flush gently with 0.5 mL and watch for blanching.

Infusion Atlas Unit

0.5–0.95 NS with heparin 1–2 units/mL at 1 mL/hr, started right away. Below 1 mL/hr, blood tends to back up and clot.

Other guidelines: 1 unit/mL at 0.5–1 mL/hr; 0.5 units/mL for preterm; 2 units/mL at 0.3–0.5 mL/hr.

Use it for sampling and pressure only; nothing but the heparinized flush runs through it.

Don't place if Atlas

Uncorrectable bleeding disorder, poor perfusion or collateral flow in that limb, local infection, limb malformation, or previous surgery there.

Sources: Atlas ch. 33, Peripheral Arterial Cannulation; Leeds Teaching Hospitals neonatal PAL guideline; Safer Care Victoria; CAHS WA; Ashford and St Peter's.

Pick the pressor

BP = CO × SVR. Is it the pipes, the pump, or both?

A hypotensive baby who is warm with bounding pulses and flash refill is not reassuring; that's the vasodilated picture and needs tone.

Decision tree

  1. Bed loose (warm, low diastolic, wide pulse pressure) → norepinephrine (or dopamine)
  2. Pump weak (poor contractility on echo, cool) → dobutamine
  3. Pump weak and afterload high (high SVR, PPHN, post-op) → milrinone; add a vasopressor if BP sags
  4. Both failing, unstable → epinephrine
  5. Still low on catecholamines → vasopressin; think hydrocortisone

Source: your Choosing a Pressor study guide (N5537 practice questions).

Hemodynamic profiles

Read six things together, not one BP: diastolic BP, pulse pressure, perfusion index, SVR, cardiac output, and tissue O₂ (StO₂). All normally sit between the 10th and 90th centiles.

ProfilePatternThink
Vasodilatory↓DBP, ↓SVR; ↑PP, PI, COWarm shock (sepsis, SIRS)
Vasoconstrictor↑DBP, ↑SVR; ↓PP, PI, COCold shock

L-R shunt↑CO, ↑PP; ↓DBP, ↓SVRDiastolic run-off (PDA)

Two babies with the same low diastolic can need opposite treatment: vasodilatory (low SVR, high output) needs a vasoconstrictor; volume-depleted (high SVR, low output) needs volume.

Infusion check: epinephrine 0.2 mcg/kg/min for an hour is about one resuscitation bolus (10 mcg/kg).

Using it

  • High BP: ≥95th percentile on 3 separate occasions. Above the 95th, watch closely; above the 99th, investigate and consider treating.
  • First days of life: BP rises on its own with postnatal age, birth weight, and GA. A BP that fails to rise, or falls, needs a look. Readings below the 10th or above the 90th percentile are common in babies who look well.
  • "MAP ≥ GA in weeks": the review calls this rule not physiologically based, with little evidence and no link to better outcomes. Judge BP with perfusion and the whole picture.
  • Measuring: an arterial line is the gold standard. A cuff measures the MAP and calculates systolic and diastolic, so compare MAP. Use the right arm; cuff bladder about 40% of arm circumference wide, covering 80–100% of it.

Dopamine and epinephrine change with dose. At the top of the dopamine range you're adding afterload, not inotropy.

Choosing between two

Dobutamine vs dopamineDobutamine for output without clamping the bed. If dopamine causes tachycardia or rising afterload with no better perfusion, switch strategy; don't climb the dose.
Milrinone vs dobutamineMilrinone when afterload is the problem or you need lusitropy (high SVR, PPHN, post-op low output). It drops BP; dobutamine is safer when pressure has no room to fall.
Norepinephrine vs epinephrineNorepinephrine for tone with a normal heart (warm septic bed). Epinephrine when you need tone and squeeze.
When vasopressinWhen catecholamines stop working (receptors downregulate in acidotic shock). Raises SVR without raising PVR or HR, so it fits PPHN and CDH.
When steroidsRefractory hypotension with suspected adrenal insufficiency (low cortisol under stress). Hydrocortisone restores response to catecholamines.

Source: your Choosing a Pressor study guide (N5537 practice questions).

Treatment algorithm

  1. Is perfusion adequate? If yes, permissive hypotension. Check NIRS, urine output, lactate, and echo, not just the cuff.
  2. Identify the phenotype. Low SVR (warm, wide pulse pressure, low diastolic) → dopamine per the lecture. Low output (cool, narrow pulse pressure) → dobutamine. Mixed → epinephrine if output is low, norepinephrine if SVR is low.
  3. If refractory, add by problem. Norepinephrine for vasoplegia; epinephrine for poor contractility; vasopressin if catecholamine-resistant; milrinone for low output with pulmonary hypertension.
  4. Still refractory: hydrocortisone.
  5. Reassess with echo, NIRS, urine output, and lactate; watch for too much vasoconstriction (cool extremities, rising lactate).
  6. Wean catecholamines first, keep milrinone while pulmonary hypertension persists, and wean vasopressin last in vasoplegia.

If BP improves but perfusion worsens, you may be over-constricting.

⚑ The lecture makes dopamine the default first drug for vasoplegia. Much current practice uses phenotype-specific first agents (such as norepinephrine or epinephrine for septic shock) instead, partly over the shunt and afterload concerns the lecture itself names. Your study guide flagged this; follow your unit's protocol.

⚑ One practice question (case 5) keys dopamine for cold shock with high SVR. That conflicts with the afterload-reduction logic the same document teaches; physiology favors an inodilator plus volume. Know the keyed answer for the exam.

Read a blood gas

Four steps Course

  1. pH: below 7.35 acidemic, above 7.45 alkalemic.
  2. PaCO₂: respiratory? It's the real-time ventilation marker.
  3. HCO₃⁻ and base excess: metabolic?
  4. Compensation, then the bedside: does it fit the work of breathing, oxygen need, and perfusion?

CO₂ moves opposite pH → respiratory. HCO₃⁻ moves with pH → metabolic.

Neonatal values Course

pH7.35–7.45 (above 7.30 may be acceptable in selected contexts)
PaCO₂35–45 mmHg
HCO₃⁻22–26 mEq/L
Base excess−2 to +2; more negative than −4 to −6 needs close attention
PaO₂Only in context: GA, age, FiO₂, support. A normal PaO₂ on 100% is not reassuring.

Venous samples underestimate PaO₂ significantly. Preterm infants tolerate lower PaO₂ targets than term.

Compensation Course

UncompensatedAbnormalStill normal

Lungs adjust CO₂ quickly; kidneys adjust HCO₃⁻ over several days. A normal pH does not mean a normal gas.

Mixed vs compensated: in compensation, the other value shifts to pull pH back toward normal. If both values push toward acid (high CO₂ and low HCO₃⁻), that's a mixed acidosis, not compensation.

⚑ The Pat Thomas guide says the kidneys "increase H+ retention" to compensate for respiratory acidosis. That's backwards: the kidneys excrete more H⁺ and hold onto HCO₃⁻, which is how Module 2 describes it.

Choosing a vent mode

ACEvery breath fully supported. No or poor spontaneous effort: respiratory failure, apnea, extreme prematurity. Risks hyperventilation and dependency.
SIMVSet breaths synced to effort, spontaneous breaths between. Weaning, low support, backup during heavy sedation (PPHN, cooling). Spontaneous volumes vary.
Volume guaranteeAdjusts pressure breath by breath to hit a set volume. Unstable compliance (hydrops, chylothorax). Guards against volutrauma.
Pressure controlCaps the pressure; volume varies with compliance. RDS, barotrauma risk, severe BPD. A stiffer lung gets a smaller breath.
HFOVTiny volumes, open-lung strategy. PIE and air leak, PPHN, severe hypoxemia.
HFJVSmall fast bursts; a conventional vent supplies PEEP. PIE, severe air leak, refractory hypercapnia.

When Course

Preterm <32 wk, VLBW and ELBW, GI anomalies or surgery, and NPO longer than 3–5 days.

Day 1 Course

D10 at 60–80 mL/kg/d, amino acids 2–3 g/kg/d, lipids 1 g/kg/d.

Amino acids Course

Start within 24 hours. The amino acid slide says start at 3 g/kg/d and advance 0.5 g/kg/d to a goal of 3–4, max 4. (The day-1 slide says 2–3.)

Card Start 2–3, advance 0.5–1.5/d, goal 3–4.

Bilirubin, 35 weeks and up

Phototherapy and exchange thresholds are hour-specific, by gestational age and whether neurotoxicity risk factors are present. They're curves, not single numbers, so plot them:

Neurotoxicity risk factors AAP 2022

Gestational age <38 weeks; albumin <3.0 g/dL; isoimmune hemolytic disease, G6PD deficiency, or other hemolysis; sepsis; significant clinical instability in the past 24 hours. Any of these lowers the thresholds.

Screening

Look at every newborn for jaundice at least every 12 hours. Jaundice before 24 hours: TcB or TSB now. Otherwise check at 24–48 hours or before discharge.

Get a TSB if the TcB is above the phototherapy threshold, within 3 mg/dL of it, or ≥15. Treatment decisions use the TSB; don't subtract the direct. If direct is more than 50% of the TSB, call an expert.

A rise of ≥0.3 mg/dL/hr in the first 24 hours suggests hemolysis: get a DAT. After 24 hours, ≥0.2 mg/dL/hr counts as a rapid rise. A TSB at the phototherapy line before 24 hours also points to hemolysis.

Phototherapy

Intensive = narrow-spectrum blue LED, ≥30 µW/cm²/nm at 460–490 nm. A bili blanket is not intensive. Keep feeds going.

Stop when the TSB is at least 2 mg/dL below the threshold where you started. Rebound TSB about 24 hours later, or at 6–12 hours if phototherapy started before 48 hours of age.

Escalation of care AAP 2022

TSB within 2 mg/dL of the exchange threshold = emergency

Manage in the NICU with intensive phototherapy and PO plus IV hydration. STAT: total and direct bilirubin, CBC, albumin, chemistry, type and screen, ABO/Rh, DAT, reticulocyte count. TSB every 2 hours until below the escalation level.

Exchange

TSB at or above the exchange threshold: urgent exchange. If the TSB falls below it while you prepare and there are no signs of intermediate or advanced acute bilirubin encephalopathy, you may hold the exchange, continue intensive phototherapy, and check every 2 hours.

Prolonged jaundice

Beyond 3–4 weeks breastfed or 2 weeks formula-fed: total and direct bilirubin and the newborn screen.

How to read an x-ray

Remember RIPE ABCDEFG.

R: RotationClavicle equal from spine
I: InspirationT9 expansion. Divide in 3 zones; look at each zone for symmetry, vascular markings, air in lung fields or outside. Fluid or blood causes increased opacity.
P: ProjectionAP, left lateral, cross table
E: ExposureLeft hemidiaphragm visible to spine. Vertebrae visible behind heart.
A: AirwayTrachea and bronchus: size, shape, shadow, site. Pushing trachea: pleural effusion or tension pneumothorax. Pulling trachea: consolidation with lobar collapse.
B: BonesFractures or change in bone density? Extra or missing? Lateral deviations?
C: Circulation / CardiacSite: left or right. Size: less than 1/2 the transthoracic diameter (is the largest diameter of the heart less than 1/2 the largest diameter of the thorax?). Shape: apex pointing to the left? Shadows: any change in density. Borders: clear or well defined?
D: DiaphragmOutline should be clear and smooth. Subdiaphragmatic air? (pneumoperitoneum)
E: EquipmentKnow your lines (below)
F: Featureless featuresLoss of normal markings in chest or abdomen. Too much air, too little, or no air (physiologic in origin).
G: Gut and gasesStomach: left upper quadrant, variable amount of air. Small bowel: intraluminal gas usually minimal within multiple tight loops. Large bowel: mix of gas and stool. Any fixed loops? Abnormally large gas areas?
